On the abelian square avoidance in words over a ternary alphabeta geometric approach

Veikko Keränen15 October 2026

Background

A word is called aa2f (almost abelian square-free) if it contains no abelian square uv as a factor, except that the shortest squares, those of period 1 (|u| = |v| = 1), are allowed.

The aa2f problem on three letters was originally posed by Sami Mäkelä in his M.Sc. thesis Patterns in words (in Finnish), University of Turku, 2002. Whether there exists an infinite word over a ternary alphabet that avoids all abelian squares of period greater than 1 is still an open problem.

A partial solution was given by M. Rao and M. Rosenfeld in 2015 in their paper “On Mäkelä’s Conjectures: deciding if a morphic word avoids long abelian-powers”. The authors show that abelian squares of period greater than 5 are avoidable on the ternary alphabet. The result also appears in Rosenfeld’s doctoral thesis (ENS de Lyon, 2017, Theorem 3.11). The same authors also answered Mäkelä’s companion question on abelian cubes negatively: abelian cubes of period at least 2 cannot be avoided in infinite binary words (Math. Comp. 85 (2016), 3051–3060).

Here we approach the subject from a geometric perspective. We show that the problem can be tackled in a way that is perhaps new, and unconventional within combinatorics on words, and that may prove fruitful in applications.

The construction

Consider an abelian square-free (a2f) word w over the four-letter alphabet {a, b, c, d}. In the sequel, we assume, for construction purposes, that w does not start with the letter d. Replace every occurrence of the letter d by the letter (a, b, or c) located immediately to its left. The replacement, or substitution, of d thus depends on the context. It produces a word w′ over the ternary alphabet {a, b, c}.

Next, replace each pair of identical adjacent letters by a single indivisible double-width instance of that letter, written ȧ, ḃ, ċ for a, b, c, respectively. If the same letter occurs three times in a row, do this, by convention, only for the leftmost pair. Denote the word obtained in this way by w″.

We agree that the standard letters a, b, and c, together with their three indivisible double-width versions ȧ, ḃ, ċ, are equivalent in terms of their calculated counts. In a sense, single-atom and two-atom molecules are treated as identical, except for their geometric nature, namely their length: a two-atom molecule (letter) is twice as long as a single atom (letter), so each occurrence of a double-width letter has length 2. In this sense we are still discussing words formed over a three-letter alphabet.

Consequently, when we ask whether an abelian square uv occurs as a factor, the lengths |u| and |v| are measured in the usual way, except that each occurrence of an indivisible double-width letter counts twice towards the length but only once towards the corresponding component of the Parikh (frequency) vector.

Algebraic formulation

Algebraically, we are dealing with words over the six-letter alphabet Γ = {a, b, c, ȧ, ḃ, ċ}, where ȧ, ḃ, ċ denote the indivisible double-width versions of a, b, c. Although Γ has six letters, its abelian structure is ternary. Two simple maps carry this structure:

  • a projection π: Γ → {a, b, c} that forgets the width, so that the Parikh vector, computed after the projection, has only three components;
  • a width function that gives the letters a, b, c the length 1 and the letters ȧ, ḃ, ċ the length 2. The sum of the widths of the letters of a word u is its geometric length, written ‖u‖ here and simply |u| in the rest of this note.

Both maps are captured by a single morphism into the group (ℤ4, +). For a letter x ∈ {a, b, c}, let ex be the standard basis vector (unit vector) of ℤ3 that represents the Parikh vector of x. It has a 1 in the position of x and 0 in the other positions:

Φ(a) = ea = (1, 0, 0),   Φ(b) = eb = (0, 1, 0),   Φ(c) = ec = (0, 0, 1).

Extended to words as a morphism, Φ: {a, b, c}* → (ℤ3, +) is the ordinary Parikh map, Φ(u) = Ψ(u). Now define Φ′: Γ* → (ℤ4, +) on the letters by Φ′(x) = (Φ(x), 1) and Φ′(ẋ) = (Φ(x), 2), and extend it to words as a morphism, so that Φ′(uv) = Φ′(u) + Φ′(v):

For every word u over Γ we then have Φ′(u) = (Ψ(π(u)), ‖u‖): the projected Parikh vector, followed by the geometric length. Two words u and v are Φ′-equivalent, written u ≈Φ′ v, if Φ′(u) = Φ′(v), that is, if they have the same projected Parikh vector and the same geometric length. A factor uv of w″ with u ≈Φ′ v is exactly an abelian square in the geometric sense used in this note, and the lengths |u| and |v| used for it are geometric lengths. In the terminology of Pirillo and Varricchio, it is a square modulo Φ′.

For ordinary words over {a, b, c} with Φ(x) = ex, the same definitions give the usual abelian equivalence, and the length is already determined by the Parikh vector. Here the extra last coordinate is essential: a double-width letter counts once in the Parikh vector but twice in the length. Adding a coordinate that records length is a standard device; with the constant 1 in the last coordinate, it expresses the equal-length (uniform) condition used by Rao and Rosenfeld. We use the width in its place.

The notion of a power modulo a morphism seems to originate in G. Pirillo and S. Varricchio, On uniformly repetitive semigroups, Semigroup Forum 49(1) (1994), 125–129. Pirillo also treats kth powers modulo a morphism in Chapter 4 (Repetitive Mappings and Morphisms) of M. Lothaire, Combinatorics on Words, Cambridge University Press, 1997 (first published 1983).

Claim and proof

Claim. Allowing indivisible double-width letters, the geometrically formed word w″ over the ternary alphabet {a, b, c} is abelian square-free (a2f), that is, it contains no square modulo Φ′; in other words, geometrically it does not even contain abelian squares of period 1.

Proof. In this proof, lengths such as |u″| are geometric lengths; for factors of w, which has no double-width letters, they are ordinary lengths. We argue indirectly. Assume that an abelian square u″v″, with |u″| = |v″|, occurs in w″. Let the Parikh vectors of u″ and v″ be Ψ(u″) = (|u″|a, |u″|b, |u″|c) and Ψ(v″) = (|v″|a, |v″|b, |v″|c), respectively. Since u″v″ is an abelian square by assumption, Ψ(u″) = Ψ(v″).

By the construction of w″ from w ∈ {a, b, c, d}*, a factor uv with |u| = |v| = |u″| = |v″| occurs in w, in the same position as u″v″ occurs in w″. Moreover, dealing now with four-component Parikh vectors, we have Ψ(u) = (|u″|a, |u″|b, |u″|c, |u|d) and Ψ(v) = (|v″|a, |v″|b, |v″|c, |v|d). Because Ψ(u″) = Ψ(v″), we obtain Ψ(v) = (|u″|a, |u″|b, |u″|c, |v|d), and furthermore

(1)
|u| = |u″|a + |u″|b + |u″|c + |u|dand|v| = |u″|a + |u″|b + |u″|c + |v|d.

Since |u| = |v|, the right-hand sides of (1) are equal, which yields |v|d = |u|d. Hence Ψ(u) = Ψ(v), and thus uv is an abelian square in w. This contradicts the abelian square-freeness of w.
The claim therefore holds. ∎
