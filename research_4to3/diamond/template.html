Diamond walks of g85, g98 and g109 The tetrahedral walk of “Bending the string” for g85(a), g98(a), the twelve words g109(a) and the squares g²(a), with loops, factors and pseudo mirror images

V. KeränenWorking notes, 8 November 2026Drawn with Claude Code

Every letter is a step of length 1 from the centre of a regular tetrahedron towards one of its vertices: a points up, and b, c, d point down to the three base vertices. This is panel (E) of “Bending the string”, following E. Jensen and V. Keränen, 4 Letter Walks in Diamond Lattice (2004). The walk starts at the origin P0, and Pk is the point reached after the first k letters. The four step vectors add up to zero and that is their only relation, so Pk records the Parikh vector of the prefix up to adding the same number to all four counts.

Two facts make the picture useful. Loops: the walk returns to a point it has visited before, Pi = Pj, exactly when the factor wi+1⋯wj contains every letter equally often; its length is then a multiple of 4. Abelian squares: wi+1⋯wi+2p is an abelian square exactly when Pi+p is the midpoint of Pi and Pi+2p, so the walks below have no such midpoints.

2 · Pseudo mirror images

The 24 permutations π of {a, b, c, d} are exactly the symmetries of the regular tetrahedron: an even permutation turns it, an odd one reflects it in a plane. So the walk of a recoloured factor π(u) is the walk of u moved by that symmetry. Reading u backwards gives the reversal ũ; its walk is the walk of u reflected through the midpoint of its two ends, a point reflection. Call π(ũ) a pseudo mirror image of u. For a picked factor u, the box above lists every occurrence in the word of π(u) and π(ũ), and says whether its walk is congruent to that of u by a rotation or by a mirror image. A point reflection in space is itself a mirror map, so π(ũ) is a rotated copy when π is odd and a mirror copy when π is even. Click an occurrence to draw it in violet.

A factor that is its own pseudo mirror image, u = π(ũ), is a pseudo palindrome; with π = id it is an ordinary palindrome. Applying the rule twice gives u = π²(u), so π is the identity or an involution: one of the six transpositions or the three double transpositions. An odd pseudo palindrome has a middle letter x with π(x) = x; an even one has a middle pair xπ(x) with π(x) ≠ x. Its walk is mapped onto itself, with the two ends swapped, by a congruence that fixes the midpoint M of the ends:

  • π = id: the point reflection through M;
  • π a transposition (xy): a half-turn about an axis through M, parallel to the edge from vertex x to vertex y of the tetrahedron;
  • π a double transposition (xy)(zt): a mirror image in a plane through M, perpendicular to the line joining the midpoints of the edges xy and zt.

The box “Pseudo palindromes of the walk” lists, for each centre of the word, the longest pseudo palindrome around it, longest first. Clicking one picks it; the box “The factor between two points” then shows it split at its centre, and the walk marks M. Picked factors and loops get the tag pseudo palindrome as well. The longest one in g98(a) is the odd 55-letter factor w40⋯w94,

dcdadbdcbdbabdbcbacbcdbabdc d bdcacdbcbacbcdcacdcbdcdadbd

with middle letter d and π = (bc), so its walk is unchanged by a half-turn about an axis through M. The same factor is depicted in V. Keränen, Structures of g98: some forbidden factors (2003, 2026).

4 · A note on DNA and RNA

DNA and RNA are words over four letters too: A, C, G, T (U in RNA). The two strands of DNA run in opposite directions and pair A with T and C with G, so the partner strand of u is its reverse complement. Writing a, b, c, d for A, C, G, T, the reverse complement is the pseudo mirror image π(ũ) with π = (ad)(bc), a double transposition. So the following ideas of this page have a direct meaning in bioinformatics:

  • Watson–Crick palindromes, u = π(ũ) with π = (AT)(CG), are the pseudo palindromes with this π. They read the same on both strands, like the EcoRI restriction site GAATTC. As π fixes no letter they always have even length, and in the walk they have a mirror plane through M. In combinatorics on words they are the ϑ-palindromes of de Luca and De Luca [5].
  • Hairpins (stem-loops) of RNA and cruciforms of DNA have the form u v π(ũ): a pseudo palindrome with a short spacer v in the middle, where the strand folds back and pairs with itself. RNA also pairs G with U (the wobble pair), which loosens the rule. Predicting such folds is the subject of RNA secondary structure programs like ViennaRNA [7].
  • Tandem repeats are squares uu, mostly with a few mutations. Unlike the words on this page, real DNA is full of squares and abelian squares, that is, midpoints of its walk; programs like Tandem Repeats Finder [6] search for approximate ones.
  • The walk itself is essentially the Z-curve of Zhang and Zhang [1], which also steps towards the vertices of a regular tetrahedron; its three coordinates measure purines against pyrimidines, amino against keto bases and A+T against G+C. Its drift shows changes of composition, such as the GC skew that changes sign at the replication origin of a bacterial genome. Older relatives are the one-dimensional DNA walk of Peng et al. [2] and the chaos game representation of Jeffrey [3]. A loop of the walk is a window with equal amounts of A, C, G and T.

The page itself takes only g85, g98 and g109; a DNA version would need an input box for any sequence, a coarser drawing for long genomes and searches that allow mismatches. Sequences can be taken from GenBank at NCBI; Gusfield's book [4] covers the string algorithms behind such searches.

Such searches could also serve AI agents. In the Virtual Biotech of Zhang et al. [10], agents built on Claude use over a hundred database tools to assess drug targets and clinical trials. Their drugs are antibodies and small molecules. For nucleic-acid drugs, such as antisense oligonucleotides, siRNAs and CRISPR guide RNAs, a sequence tool would check that a designed strand has no long Watson–Crick palindromes or hairpins, so that it does not fold back on itself.

Complementarity and computational universality: the twin-shuffle language

Complementarity is not only a symmetry; it can compute anything. Following A. Salomaa [8], write the four DNA letters as bits with and without a bar: A = 0, G = 1, T = 0, C = 1. Purines are unbarred, pyrimidines barred, and the bar is the Watson–Crick complement, with double bars cancelling. A shuffle of two words interleaves them without changing the order of the letters within either word. The twin-shuffle language TS consists of all shuffles of a binary word y with its barred copy y. For example, 0011 and 0101 are in TS (y = 01), but 0110 is not. Membership is easy to test: delete the barred letters of z, then separately delete the unbarred ones and remove the bars; z is in TS exactly when the two results are equal. TS is thus a single, fixed and simple language, a word interleaved with its complement, just as a DNA double strand is a strand paired with its complement.

The surprising fact, due to J. Engelfriet and G. Rozenberg [9], is that TS is universal: for every recursively enumerable language L, that is, for every language accepted by some Turing machine, there is a generalized sequential machine g with L = g(TS). A gsm is a finite automaton that writes output while it reads input; on its own it is very weak. Equivalently, L is obtained from a version of TS over a larger alphabet by intersecting with a regular language and erasing the auxiliary letters. So all the power of a Turing machine already lies in the complementarity relation. Only the input–output format g changes from task to task, while TS, like Watson–Crick complementarity, always stays the same. Salomaa [8] uses this to explain why so many quite different models of DNA computing turn out to be Turing universal: every one of them contains complementarity in some form. Two ways of reading a double strand show the link:

  • Reading the upper and lower strands alternately, letter by letter, gives a word of TS.
  • Reading the upper strand x forwards and then the lower strand backwards, following the orientation of the molecule, gives xx̃. This word lies in the reverse twin-shuffle language RTS, the shuffles of y with ỹ, for which the universality theorem also holds.

The word xx̃ is exactly a Watson–Crick palindrome in the sense above: π(ũ) = u with π = (AT)(CG). So the walks of the double strands read around their ends are precisely the even pseudo palindromes of that π, the walks with a mirror plane through their midpoint.

Hairpins are universal too. Engelfriet and Rozenberg [9, Theorem 11] show that every recursively enumerable language is obtained from the hairpin language of all words w#w̃ in the same way: intersect it with a simple deterministic linear language and erase the auxiliary letters. Here w is the stem, the marker # plays the role of the loop, and w̃ is the reverse complement of the stem, so these words are hairpins u v π(ũ) with π = (AT)(CG). Among the words that have all their unbarred letters before their barred letters, the hairpin words are exactly the Dyck words. In a Dyck word each unbarred letter is an opening bracket, its barred copy is the matching closing bracket, and the pairs are nested. This gives a contrast between two kinds of pairing:

  • Nested pairing, as in Dyck words and in the dot-bracket notation of RNA secondary structures predicted by ViennaRNA [7], belongs to the context-free languages. By the Chomsky–Schützenberger theorem, every context-free language is obtained from a Dyck language by intersecting with a regular language and erasing letters.
  • Parallel pairing, as in TS, where the i-th unbarred letter is matched with the i-th barred one, gives all recursively enumerable languages in the same way. In the words of [9], the twin shuffles play for the recursively enumerable languages the role that the Dyck languages play for the context-free ones.

Crossing pairs, the pseudoknots of RNA, are exactly what the standard folding programs leave out. The matching decision result [9, Theorem 17] is that it is undecidable whether a given simple deterministic linear language contains a hairpin word ww̃.

The equality machine. Engelfriet and Rozenberg [9, Section 7] also give TS a machine picture. An equality machine is a finite automaton with two write-only tapes. It accepts when, at the end, the two tapes hold the same word. Read a DNA word as a list of instructions: A and G write 0 and 1 on tape 1, and T and C write 0 and 1 on tape 2. The words of TS are exactly the instruction lists that leave the two tapes equal. Equivalently, TS is the equality language Eq(α, β) = {z : α(z) = β(z)} of two morphisms:

  • α keeps A and G and erases T and C;
  • β erases A and G and maps T to A and C to G.

Equality languages of morphisms are the solution sets of instances of the Post correspondence problem. So maps of the same kind as g85, g98 and g109 are already where undecidability begins. Nondeterministic equality machines accept exactly the recursively enumerable languages, and deterministic ones with a single state accept exactly the equality languages of morphisms [9, Theorem 18].

TS also has a simple picture in the walk. Write tA, tC, tG, tT for the four step vectors (ta, tb, tc, td on this page). A word z of TS is y interleaved with its complement, so every A of y (a 0) is matched by a T (a 0) and every G by a C:

#A = #T = p and #C = #G = q.

The walk of z starts at P0 = 0 and ends at the sum of its steps,

ptA + ptT + qtC + qtG = p(tA + tT) + q(tC + tG) = (p − q)(tA + tT),

because the four steps add up to zero, so tC + tG = −(tA + tT). Hence the walk of every word of TS ends on one and the same line ℓ: the line through the start in the direction tA + tT.

  • The end point is the lattice point p − q = #A − #C steps along ℓ, each of length |tA + tT| = 2/√3. For example, AATT (y = 00) ends 2 steps along ℓ, GGCC (y = 11) ends 2 steps the other way, and ATGC (y = 01) returns to the start, so it is a loop.
  • Geometrically, ℓ is the line through the midpoints of the opposite tetrahedron edges AT and CG. In the coordinates of the Z-curve [1] it is the z-axis, which measures A + T against G + C.
  • Only the end point is pinned down. In between, the walk may wander off ℓ, since a prefix of z need not be balanced.
  • The condition is necessary, not sufficient: AGCT has the right counts but is not in TS, because its unbarred part AG = 01 differs from its barred part CT = 10.

The walk thus sees only the abelian shadow of TS, the counts #A = #T and #C = #G. That shadow can be checked by simple counting. For a single pair of letters it is the context-free language {#a = #b}, Example 2 of [9]. All the computing power of TS lies in the order of the letters, which no abelian view, such as the end point of the walk, can see.

Why |tA + tT| = 2/√3

The four step vectors tA, tC, tG, tT (ta, tb, tc, td on this page) are unit vectors: each points from the centre O of a regular tetrahedron to one of its vertices A, C, G, T, and the tetrahedron is scaled so that this distance is 1. In the coordinates of the page, tA = (0, 1, 0), and tC, tG, tT point down to the base, each with height −1/3.

  1. Each step has length 1, so tA·tA = tC·tC = tG·tG = tT·tT = 1.
  2. Any two different steps have dot product −1/3. The four vectors add up to zero, so the square of their sum is zero:

    0 = |tA + tC + tG + tT|² = (sum of the four |tx|²) + 2 · (sum of the six products tx·ty).

    By the symmetry of the tetrahedron all six products have the same value c, so 0 = 4 + 12c and c = −1/3. This is the tetrahedral angle arccos(−1/3) ≈ 109.47° between any two steps. In the coordinates above, tA·tT is simply the height of tT, −1/3.
  3. The length of the sum:

    |tA + tT|² = |tA|² + |tT|² + 2 tA·tT = 1 + 1 − 2/3 = 4/3, so |tA + tT| = √(4/3) = 2/√3 ≈ 1.1547.

    The same holds for every pair of letters; in particular tC + tG = −(tA + tT) has the same length.

Geometric meaning. The midpoint of the edge AT is MAT = (tA + tT)/2, at distance 1/√3 from O, and the midpoint of the opposite edge CG is MCG = (tC + tG)/2 = −MAT. So ℓ passes through MCG, O and MAT, and one step along ℓ, |tA + tT| = 2/√3, is the distance between the midpoints of the opposite edges AT and CG. For comparison, an edge has length |tA − tT| = √(1 + 1 + 2/3) = √(8/3) ≈ 1.633, so the step along ℓ is the edge length divided by √2.
